# Owner voice

A style reference describing how the repo owner writes and in which contexts she writes that way, for an agent drafting on her behalf; the Quote bank section holds verbatim examples to model.
Built from the `college-work` folder: about 420 files inventoried, roughly 280 judged hers, read by seven parallel readers working disjoint slices.
Everything here dates from 2016 to 2021, so it describes student-era writing, not current writing.
The voice lives in what is said first and how uncertainty is admitted, not in the splices.
Where this document names a form rather than an observed habit, that form is the one the owner has chosen for writing done on her behalf.

## Contexts

### Formal report and graded paper

Research papers, legal memos, incident reports, policy documents, the URSP grant proposal. Written when a grade or outside reader is attached and the genre is explicitly "a paper". A work report for a client or a boss takes this profile: no contractions, "the team" or "the investigation" as the actor, process narration kept in the third person.

Register is measured and institutional. First person retreats to "the team", "the investigation", or "we"; jokes and sarcasm disappear entirely. Sentences run 20 to 35 words with subordinate clauses. Contractions are suppressed. Connectors are used correctly and often: "However", "Therefore", "In addition", "Furthermore". Intensifiers drop away; hedges shrink to "may", "likely", "it is thought that".

Passive constructions concentrate here and are rare in every other context ("were indicted by", "is granted"), the register leaking in rather than a choice; elsewhere she writes actor-first by instinct. In her voice a formal sentence leads with the actor when the actor is known and matters ("the DOJ indicted"), with "the team" or "the investigation" standing in for "I"; the passive is kept for an actor who is unknown ("data was exfiltrated from three hosts"), procedurally irrelevant ("access is granted through the gateway"), or deliberately unnamed.

Structure is templated: heading blocks (Executive Summary, Purpose/Scope, Roles and Responsibilities), an auto-generated Word table of contents, a one-line topic statement up front, a closing summary paragraph rather than a trailing stop. Body paragraphs run three to six sentences, one claim with its citations each, 150 to 300 words where no subheading breaks them. Every substantive piece ends with a Bibliography, References, or SOURCES list, including short ones. Citations are bracket-numbered `[1]`; the APA-adjacent inline form of the student papers is historical. Punctuation is cleaner here than anywhere else; these are the proofread documents. Serial comma is inconsistent within a single document.

- "The investigation found several successful attacks, some of which were able to exfiltrate data, from many different sources." (IA473_Brandon-Paul_Final-Report.docx)
- "Alleged Chinese hackers were indicted by the United States Department of Justice (DOJ) for hacking companies Siemens and Trimble." (FinalPaper-BrandonPaul.docx)
- "Though the advent of small and affordable software defined radios has lowered the barrier of entry, there are still significant hurdles to overcome before a functioning testing environment can be created." (2020_URSP_Brandon-Paul_Proposal_Draft-1.docx)

Even here the personal register can break through at the very end, and it sits right when the reader is a person rather than an institution: "I learned so much about cellular infrastructure and I intend to continue this research long past this project's finish date." (URSP Final Report.docx)

### Technical lab writeup and process narrative

Assignments reporting work she did: builds, configs, forensics labs, troubleshooting logs. The dominant mode across the technical courses.

She narrates her own reasoning live rather than reporting a result. The recurring shape is hypothesis, failure, next hypothesis: "I thought X. However, that was not the issue. Next I thought Y." Sentences are medium and chained with "So", "But", "However", "Then", "Once", "After", often as sentence openers. Contractions are used freely. Field terms go undefined (pfSense, NIC, DHCP, forest, FSMO, FTK, Nmap), and choices get a stated reason, frequently a personal one, with the technical reason standing beside it rather than in place of it.

Structure: bolded short headers, often phrased as questions ("Whis is this useful?"), each answered in two to four sentences; numbered steps; inline figure references ("Version information can be found in figure 1.1."); command and credential blocks pasted with comment headers. Comma splices are common, carrying the short-after-long beat.

- "I used server 2016, because I hate the 2012 interface." (IA462_BrandonPaul_Assignment 2.docx)
- "So before I was able to do anything, I had to uninstall the most recent update and hope that it did not break anything else in the process." (IA-428_Brandon-Paul_Assignment-3.docx)
- "Things are going slow, and we are having issues actually getting an exploit to work." (IA-445_Brandon-Paul_Project-Progress-Report.docx)

### Short-answer worksheet and exam response

Quiz fields, module Q&A, fill-in lab worksheets, prompts answered under time pressure.

Answer-only. The question supplies the context, so the answer does not restate it. One clause to three sentences, sometimes a bare value or command. No intro, no wrap-up. Where the prompt asks for judgment she writes "I believe". Where it asks for procedure she switches to a hypothetical voice built on "would": "I would start by...", "At this point/stage...". Jargon density is high, hedge density low. Grammar loosens visibly under the clock: dropped copulas, dropped subjects, lowercase sentence starts. That is pace, not voice. Blank answers are left blank rather than filled with waffle.

- "I would start by notifying any required parties that a preliminary investigation of a potential incident has begun." (IA-455_Discussions.docx)
- "True. Sending such information through HTTP is inviting failure." (L2-Lab-IdenThreatsVuln-1.docx)

### Reflective and discussion post

Course reflections, discussion boards, listening journals, class introductions. Her most consistent long-form voice.

Conversational first person addressed to a reader she assumes is the grader. She opens with a concrete anecdote or an admission of ignorance, then generalizes, then closes on a personal takeaway. Paragraphs run four to eight sentences with no headers. Sentences average 15 to 25 words, punctuated by short punchy ones placed right after a long one, and by deliberate fragments used as a beat. Asides ride inside em dashes and parentheses.

Word choice is hedge-heavy and self-aware about it ("I don't know", "I'm not sure", "I have no idea", "I imagine"), with stacked intensifiers ("extremely", "definitely", "genuinely", "literally", "really"); the honesty is the trait rather than the quantity. Contractions throughout. Self-deprecation is a repeated move: she names the gap in her knowledge before asserting an opinion. Several of these posts close with a bulleted "Questions:" list, near-universal in the 2016 WGST-202 set. Comma splices, sentence-boundary capitalization lapses, and a dropped comma before "but" run through all of it.

- "It was incredibly difficult for me to not give advice. Honestly, it might be my biggest flaw when listening to people." (COMM-225_Listening-Observations_Brandon-Paul.docx)
- "Yet, none of these ideas follow cause and effect. These aren't universal truths, they are just perception." (reflection_1.docx)
- "I have no idea what jazz "is", and the internet has not been all that useful in helping me understand a better definition." (What Is Jazz.docx)
- "I expect to learn how to digital forensic. I have so little knowledge on this topic that the previous ridiculous sentence is probably more accurate than you gave it credit for." (Class Introduction_BrandonPaul.docx)

### Notes

Two modes share this context; workers disagreed about which is typical (see Caveats).

**Transcription notes** (lecture notes, certification study guides, textbook chapters compressed): telegraphic, nearly voiceless. Noun phrases and clipped clauses, no subject in most lines, nested bullets under bold or all-caps headers, auto-generated tables of contents, no terminal periods on fragments. She is compressing someone else's material, and it reads that way. Domain terms carried over verbatim.

**Working notes and research journals** (Docker/OpenAirInterface notes, the URSP research journal, midterm cram notes, note-to-self annotations): same fragment grammar, but the voice is fully present. Dated entries, raw terminal errors pasted inline as evidence, one-line self-corrections mid-log, `Term; definition` glossary lines with a semicolon where a colon belongs, all-caps for a milestone or exasperation ("IT WORKS!!!", "QUIZ NEXT WEEK", "SHOW FISMO ROLES"), and a sarcastic one-liner dropped into a factual list with no setup. Hedging clusters here whenever she is describing her own experiment ("not sure", "I believe", "maybe"), and vanishes when she is transcribing a lecture. Abbreviations: `w/`, `w/o`, `+`, `eg.`, `mobo`. Rhetorical questions serve as section headers. Nothing here is written for another reader, and this is where the voice is most alive.

- "I don't care how many trash piles they put in the ocean, that doesn't make it an island. China needs to chill out." (9_17 Homework.docx)
- "Same error, but with the corrected file path" (Research/Journal.docx)
- "I'm not going to test that at the moment. Maybe you could get this to run on a quad-core Raspberry Pi." (Working With Open Air Interface.docx)

### Work and user-facing documentation

Help-desk tutorials, procedure docs, canned incident-response emails. Written for a stranger who has to follow it.

Plain instructional register, second-person imperative, one action per numbered step. No jargon beyond product and menu names. Contractions are used here, unlike in academic prose. Bold callouts and "NOTE:" asides. She wrote her own formatting rule for it: "Do not put punctuation after listed items i.e:" (Help Desk Training Notes.docx). Canned emails run greeting, one or two sentences of cause and fix, first-name sign-off. Sample steps: "Go to the website account.emich.edu." / "Click the Forgotten Password link." (PassWordResetGuide-1-3.docx)

- "If you have any issues for ANY reason, please call the Help Desk." (PassWordResetGuide-1-3.docx)
- "This may be an issue with your browser cache. To resolve this issue, please clear your browsing data." (CommonIncidentComments.docx)

### Personal and comic writing

Speeches, a class presentation, music reflections, an unassigned opinion essay. Written for enjoyment or performance, not a rubric. Her least filtered register.

Profanity, internet slang, deadpan gags, running jokes carried across several slides, fake citations, scare quotes. Sentences pile up with "and"/"but" chains and comma splices, the chain being the register's signature at its loosest and cut to its first "and" in anything read by others; intensifier stacking is at its heaviest here ("really", "so", "super", "incredibly", "phenomenal"). Subject-verb slips increase with enthusiasm. In one case she wrote the prose first, then re-distilled it into a bulleted outline underneath. The unassigned opinion essay is the exception in tone: no jokes, one dense unheaded paragraph, broad opening narrowed to a stated focus, closing on a proposed solution.

- "It's almost the only time in the track you can hear the bass, and it's bright, and it's super warm, and it has a lot of movement." (In Circles (Feat. Ashley Barrett).docx)
- "This is a block diagram I stole from a random NST Techsemic document I found" (Mooses.pptx)
- "Regardless of what is done, the cost of housing for an area will always match that of the people living there." (Housing Stuff.docx)

## Constant across contexts

- **Self-narrated reasoning.** She writes the process, not just the conclusion: tried X, it failed, tried Y next. Named by most readers; absent only in transcription notes, which are not her voice.
- **Named uncertainty.** She says "I'm not sure", "I have no idea", "I don't remember", "not sure", rather than bluffing or padding, and the admission appears once rather than repeating down the page. Blank answers are left blank.
- **A self-deprecating or dryly opinionated aside** that breaks the surrounding register: more often in reflections than elsewhere, dropped where the register around it is flattest, set off with em dashes or parentheses. Dashes and colons introduce lists as well, where another writer would reach for "such as". The single strongest authorship signal; several unsigned files were attributed to her on this alone.
- **Sentence-initial connectives** as paragraph and sentence transitions: "So,", "However,", "Yet,", "Then", "But". They carry into every register, a formal connector often fronting a wholly casual sentence.
- **Comma splices and run-on "and" chains**, present at every level of polish including the proposal drafts. Underneath them is the rhythm a reader actually hears, a short sentence after a long one. In work read by others the splice becomes two sentences and the chain is cut at its first "and"; in her own notes both stay.
- **Questions used as structure**: rhetorical questions as section headers, each answered in two to four sentences, and as essay openers, essay closers, and closing "Questions:" lists.
- **A closing source list** on anything substantive, however informal.
- **Structural leftovers kept, not deleted**: shelved drafts, duplicate scaffolds, near-identical saved copies.

## What shifts

- **Formality tracks the audience, not the topic.** Anything read by an institution (graded paper, legal memo, policy doc, client-facing report) drops contractions, first person, and jokes. Anything framed as showing her own work (lab writeup, reflection, journal) keeps all three even under formal headers and numbered sections.
- **Contractions** are the cleanest dial: absent in formal reports and incident-response answers, freely present everywhere else.
- **Sentence length** scales with formality: fragments in notes, single clauses in worksheets, 15 to 25 words in reflections, 20 to 35 words in formal reports.
- **Fragments** are the default unit in notes, deliberate and rare in reflective prose, absent in formal reports.
- **Hedging** is heaviest in reflective writing and in notes about her own experiments; near-zero in worksheets, transcription notes, and formal report bodies. Where hedges stack ("I think maybe it might be"), the one honest hedge is doing all the work.
- **Intensifiers** peak in personal and music writing, are muted in notes, and are absent from formal reports. One reads as her; three read as tired.

## Drift over time

The evidence is suggestive, not conclusive; slices were split by year, so each observation rests on one reader.

The 2016 material is the least register-separated: contractions, profanity, slang, and first-person opinion asides appear even in graded research papers. By 2017 and 2018 the separation is sharp. Essays and policy documents suppress contractions entirely, first-person opinion appears only where a prompt invites it, and every solo piece closes with a source list. By 2020 and 2021 the formal register is at its most polished (the URSP proposal is the most edited prose in the corpus, and later drafts show mentor revision), while the working journal from the same period is the loosest writing found. The voice markers themselves do not fade: the 2021 MGMT-202 assignments still carry a running joke, and the 2021 research journal still opens with sarcasm.

## Caveats

- Samples are student-era, 2016 to 2021. Nothing after 2021 was available, and no professional or personal correspondence beyond help-desk work documents.
- Attribution for many files rests on voice-matching alone, with no name in the file. Quotes here were preferred from named files.
- Group documents (two or more names on the title page) were tagged co-authored, low confidence. No quotes here are taken from them.
- Workers disagreed on whether notes carry voice. Two readers described notes as voiceless compressed source material; two described them as the place her sarcasm lives. Resolved above by splitting the context: transcription notes are voiceless, self-made working notes are not.
- Workers disagreed on hedging in notes. One reported "no hedges in notes, declarative even when wrong"; another reported hedging as the dominant note habit. Resolved as a function of subject: lecture content is stated flatly, her own experimental results are hedged.
- Workers disagreed on whether opinion intrudes into formal writing: the 2016 reader found first-person asides inside research papers, the 2017 reader found opinion confined to prompts that invite it. Treated as drift, not contradiction.
- Coverage gaps: spreadsheets and most PowerPoint files were not extractable, several documents were screenshots with no text, a number of files were empty, and one slice was scoped to .docx only, leaving 26 PDFs unread. Credential files, images, audio, archives, and packet captures were never opened.
- Quirks listed as recurring were each seen by at least one reader across multiple files; a few (semicolon-as-colon glossary lines, the two-pass prose-then-outline drafting habit) were seen by one reader only.
