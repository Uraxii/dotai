## Per-context profile

### Assignment essays (IA-240 intelligence/security papers, ASTR-105 planet reports)

Register/tone: informal-academic. Confident, opinionated, occasionally blunt even where the assignment is descriptive (planet reports) rather than argumentative. In the argumentative pieces (Pan Am, ELF, Diego Garcia) she takes a clear policy position and states it as a topic-sentence claim before evidence.

Sentence shape: mostly compound sentences built on "and," "but," "however," "so." Frequent sentence-initial "However," "So," "Given that." Average sentence maybe 20–28 words, with intentional short beats for emphasis: *"At any cost."* / *"Not a single day passes without proof."* (not literal — but pattern is: short 2-4 word fragments dropped after a long sentence for punch, e.g. "Even if this means solely using it for GPS navigation.")

Word choice: no contractions in the essays themselves (formal register kept for assignments) but they slip into notes/lab answers. Hedges: "likely," "unlikely," "may," "it makes sense that." Intensifiers: "extremely," "significantly," "very." Recurs on "given that," "so," "however."

Structure habits: short paragraphs (3–6 sentences), each built around one claim + citations. No headers within the essay body, but always ends with a "Bibliography"/"SOURCES:" list. Uses in-text parenthetical citations, not always exact APA form. Drafts show a rough→final pass that mostly reorders paragraphs and drops a duplicate copy-paste block rather than rewriting sentences (Assignment 3 rough draft has the whole thing pasted twice — once as topic-sentence bullets, once as flowing prose; final version keeps only the flowing-prose pass).

Punctuation/spelling quirks: consistent minor typos — "amereratuer" (amateur), "Isreal" (Israel), "bot boys" (both boys), "Mar's" for "Mars's," "loose" for lose isn't seen but "loose" homophone errors do not appear; "there" for "their" not seen but "affect/effect" swapped ("may drop those number again and effect future projects"). Double-word slips: "Miss Miss Mitchell's Comet." Comma habits: comma splices are common ("This colonization would lead directly to the Anticolonial Wave of terrorism. World War I and World War II would also establish..." — fine, but elsewhere: "So to give a more detailed example I'll begin..." — comma after "So" omitted). Overuses "however" as a paragraph-opener conjunction.

Quotes:
- "The U.S. should focus the bulk of its efforts on providing social programs for the region." (Assignment 1 - Pan Am Flight 103)
- "Right now, the U.S. does not have the people on their side." (Assignment 1 - Pan Am Flight 103)
- "There are a of couple steps ELF technology could take us." (Assignment 2 - ELF; typo "a of couple" kept verbatim)
- "This colonization would lead directly to the Anticolonial Wave of terrorism." (Evolution of Terrorism)
- "It is certain that the Middle-East will be a breeding ground for threats against the world for a long time to come." (final_brief_final)
- "KCM is extremely likely to die or significantly shrink within the next decade." (Assignment 3 Rough Draft — "die or" cut in the final version, softened to "significantly shrink")

### Personal/reflective (class introduction post)

Register/tone: casual, self-deprecating, dry humor. This is the strongest first-person voice in the slice.

Sentence shape: short, punchy, first-person. Uses contractions freely ("I've," "don't"). Comic timing via a sentence that undercuts itself.

Word choice: "pretty," "probably," "so little," hedge-heavy but self-aware about the hedging.

Quotes:
- "I expect to learn how to digital forensic. I have so little knowledge on this topic that the previous ridiculous sentence is probably more accurate than you gave it credit for." (Class Introduction_BrandonPaul.docx)
- "I hear Wireshark is pretty important." (same file)
- "I learn through doing more, so as many labs as there can be I would like." (same file — note the inverted, slightly awkward clause order; a recurring habit, not just here)

### Notes (bulleted research notes, personal working notes)

Register/tone: telegraphic, no full sentences needed, heavy on brackets/citation markers, quoting sources verbatim with her own bracket-numbered citations `[1]`.

Structure habits: colon-led labels ("Product:", "Who:", "Notes:"), bullet lists, no connective prose. Contrasts sharply with the essay register — this is thinking-out-loud, not written-for-a-reader.

Quotes:
- "Why do they not trade that much with neighboring nations?" (Brandon Paul Notes.docx — a note-to-self question, unanswered)
- "Positive trade balance; steadily increasing." (same file)
- "Good link I found about Zambia's culture, sports, religion..." (IA 240 Africa Project.docx — casual "I found" aside inside an otherwise task-oriented planning doc)

### Lab reports / technical short-answer (IA-202 risk labs, IA-427 forensics labs, exam short-answer)

Register/tone: plain, functional, first-person opinion appears only when the question explicitly asks for judgment ("I believe"). Otherwise third-person/imperative, mirroring the instructor's question phrasing back as the answer's frame.

Sentence shape: short declaratives, often sub-sentence fragments as answers ("Official Cover, Non-Official Cover"). When a question asks for reasoning, answers run 1–3 sentences, no more.

Word choice: domain jargon dense (COBIT, HIPAA, LAN/WAN, VPN, FTK, Nmap) used correctly and without hedging — contrasts with the hedge-heavy essay voice. "I believe" appears exactly where personal judgment is explicitly requested, nowhere else in this context.

## Cross-cutting observations

What stays constant: no contractions in formal writing (essays, policy docs, lab prose answers); contractions appear freely in personal/casual writing (class intro) and occasionally in bulleted notes. Citation habit is constant across every solo academic piece — she always closes with a References/Bibliography/SOURCES list, even in short 150-word notes. Recurring grammar/spelling slips persist across contexts and years: dropped copulas ("He known"), homophone/typo slips (Isreal, amereratuer), comma-splice-prone "However"/"So" sentence openers, and inverted clause order ("so as many labs as there can be I would like").

What shifts: formality goes up for anything with a grade attached (essays, policy docs, exam answers) and drops to near-conversational only in the one personal-introduction document and inside notes-to-self. First-person opinion ("I believe," "I recommend," "I am confident") appears only where a question or genre explicitly invites judgment (opinion essays, "what do you think" lab questions) — she does not volunteer personal opinion in purely descriptive assignments (planet reports stay third-person throughout).

What marks a piece as hers vs. generic: (1) the "however/so"-led sentence habit and reliance on hedged intensifiers ("extremely likely," "significantly"); (2) closing every substantive piece with a source list, even informal ones; (3) recurring specific misspellings that show up nowhere in the instructor-authored templates (those are typo-free, professionally copyedited); (4) the "topic claim → evidence → mild hedge" essay paragraph shape, distinct from the imperative, second-person "you will..." voice of every unfilled instructor lab template.
