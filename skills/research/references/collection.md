# Collection: fixing the question, planning, finding, and being refused

Steps 1 to 3. Load at step 1 and keep it open until the first document is
stored.

## Fix the question

An area of interest is not a question. "Whether to acquire the supplier" is an
area. "Does the supplier's order book survive the loss of its largest customer"
is a question, because there is something that would answer it.

Three tests a question has to pass before collection starts. They come from
GAO-12-208G, *Designing Evaluations*, chapter 2, which is written for exactly
this moment.

- **Specific.** It names the thing, the period, and the population. A question
  that could be asked of any company in any year will be answered that way.
- **Measurable.** Some observable answers it. If you cannot name one, you have
  a topic, not a question.
- **Free of its own answer.** "Why is the rollout failing" presumes the
  failure. Ask whether it is failing, then why.

Where somebody else set the question, record their exact wording beside your
restatement and settle the difference with them before working. FM 5-0's staff
study chapter puts it as a step with teeth: a problem handed to you gets
approved before you act on it. Half the failed research in the world answered
a question nobody asked. A delegated agent that cannot reach the requester
records both wordings, marks the difference, and proceeds; it does not claim an
approval it never got.

Do not write the answer down yet, even provisionally. The answer is what the
collection turns out to support.

## Plan the collection

Full mode. Two lists, written before the first document is opened.

**What would answer it.** For each candidate answer, name the observable that
would establish it. Not a topic to read about: a thing that either is or is not
the case.

**What would break it.** For each candidate answer, name what you would expect
to see if it were true and are not seeing, and what you would expect to see if
it were false. This is the half agents skip, and skipping it is how a
collection ends up containing only the material that fits.

Then order the work. GAO-20-195G, the *GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment
Guide*, chapter 9, gives the sequence that survives contact: settle purpose,
scope, and what you are measuring against first, then plan the collection, then
collect, and keep revising the plan as what you find changes it. The same
chapter is blunt about source preference: prefer a primary source, meaning the
body that created or holds the data, over anyone reporting on it.

ATP 2-22.9, *Open-Source Intelligence*, chapter 1, adds the discipline that
keeps a plan honest: turn the question into named requirements, decide which
source could satisfy each one, and keep assessing whether the collection is
actually satisfying them rather than merely running. Collection that nobody
assesses drifts into collecting whatever is easy to reach.

Two ordering rules worth following.

- Go after what discriminates first. A document that fits every candidate
  answer equally well has told you nothing, however solid it is.
- Go after what expires first. A person who is leaving, a log with a retention
  window, a page that changes weekly.

## Find candidates

Gather the candidate list before examining any of it in detail. Skimming
twenty and reading five beats reading five and never learning the other fifteen
existed.

Record where each candidate came from at the moment you find it. Retrieval
paths are cheap to note now and expensive to reconstruct later, and a candidate
you cannot relocate is one you will look for twice.

Vary where you look, not just how much. A search engine, an official register,
the originator's own publications, a regulator's filings, and a person who was
there return different material by construction, not different amounts of the
same material. ATP 2-22.9, *Open-Source Intelligence*, chapter 3, puts the same
rule as a survey step: work out what kinds of source exist for this question
before working through any one of them, and treat the survey as a product in
its own right.

## When you are refused

Full mode. Access failures are normal and they are findings. ATP 2-22.9's
collection chapter treats an unavailable source as an outcome to report back
against the requirement, not as a silent hole. Three kinds, three responses.

**The host refuses the client.** A page a browser serves and a script does not.
Try the same document from another host that carries it: another arm of the
same organization, a national archive, a library mirror. When you take a copy
instead of the original, record which host served it and what the original did,
so the reader can weigh it. No standard here says when a copy is good enough,
and this skill does not invent one.

**The document is behind a wall.** Paywalled, login-gated, or licensed. Do not
route around the wall. Cite the document by title and section from what you can
legitimately see, mark in its row that the text is not stored, and record in
item 7 that the claim rests on material you could not keep.

**The document does not exist, or nobody will give it to you.** Say so in item
7, name what you asked for and of whom, and say what the answer therefore rests
on instead. A refusal recorded is evidence about the subject. A refusal
silently dropped turns an incomplete collection into one that looks complete.

Every one of these gets a consulted-log line whatever happens. The point of
that log is that the next run does not repeat this run's dead ends.
