## Per-context profile

### Reflective/discussion posts (WGST-202: reflections, "myth" responses, chapter notes)
Register: informal-academic, first person, opinionated, conversational. Sentences run long, comma-spliced, frequently open with a connective ("So,", "However,", "Yet,", "But then"). Fragments used for emphasis ("Kinda dumb."). Contractions throughout (don't, doesn't, I've, isn't). Hedges: "I don't know", "I'm not sure", "I believe", "in my opinion". Intensifiers: "extremely", "definitely", "genuinely", "literally", "completely". Structure: opens with topic restatement, builds through personal anecdote or tangent, closes with a bulleted "Questions:" list — this closing habit is nearly universal across the WGST-202 posts. Typos: "homosecual" (myth_9), lowercase word after a period ("the internet comes from the outside, and it comes to my modem" — sentence-boundary capitalization lapses recur across contexts, not just this one). ## Cross-cutting observations

Constant across every context: contractions always present (even in the most formal SAJAR paper); comma splices and run-on sentences; rhetorical questions used as both essay-openers and essay-closers; a personal aside or hedge breaks into otherwise expository writing ("Personally, I think...", "I don't know, but..."); sentence-initial connectives ("So,", "However,", "Yet,") as paragraph transitions; assignment headers follow a "Name - Course - ID" or "Course - Name" dash-separated pattern almost everywhere she filled one in.

What shifts: formality climbs sharply for graded research papers (SAJAR "Four Layer Chaotic Encryption" draft) — contractions nearly vanish, sentences lengthen, personal asides shrink to a minimum — versus the WGST-202 reflections and CTAC-124 personal-narrative speeches, which are her least filtered writing (profanity, slang, blunt jokes). Technical lab writing is not really "her voice" at all in most files — it's copied instructional text with her name pasted on top; wherever a lab asks an open question, though, the answer reverts to the same plain, occasionally funny, occasionally typo'd voice seen elsewhere.

What marks a piece as hers vs generic: presence of a first-person aside, a rhetorical question, a joke or profanity breaking a technical register, a comma-spliced or lowercase-after-period sentence boundary, or a closing "Questions:" list. Absence of all of these (pure instructional/textbook text, blank name field, blank fill-in fields) is the strongest signal a document is template, not authored.
