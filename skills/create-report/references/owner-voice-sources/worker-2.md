## Per-context profiles

### 1. Formal academic papers (ARTH-100 Final, COSC-105 Project-01)
Register: essay-formal, third person, citation-aware (APA-ish inline: "(Baase & Henry, 2018, p. 16)"). Sentence shape: long, multi-clause, often opens with a scene-setting dependent clause ("Despite his already accomplished works..."). Structure: title/byline/course header block, then thesis-first paragraphs, no section headings, closing paragraphs synthesize rather than restate. No contractions. Word choice: "significant," "considerations," "regarding," "outweigh," mild hedges ("is thought to have," "likely painted"). Repetition as a rhetorical device: "In that short time... In that time... In that time..." (COSC-105).
Quotes:
- "The most significant risk regarding IoT devices is the increase of attack surface." (COSC-105_Capter-1-Discussion_Brandon-Paul.docx)
- "Just because something can be connected to a network does not necessarily mean it should be connected to one." (COSC-105_Capter-1-Discussion_Brandon-Paul.docx)

- "The color temperature of The River is firmly in the cool temperature range." (Discussion-4.docx)

### 2. Assignment lab-narrative / troubleshooting log (IA-428 Assignment-1/3, IA-455 Assignment 2/6-x)
Register: first person, present-tense procedural mixed with past-tense narration of what went wrong. Sentence shape: short-to-medium, often chained with "So," "But," "Once," "After." Fragments rare but chronology-driven run-ons appear ("I launched Google Chrome, which now causes my screen to flash black, so I closed Chrome and launched Microsoft Edge."). Distinctive habit: rhetorical mini-headers phrased as questions, with a recurring typo — "Whis is this useful?" appears twice as a section header, clearly meant as "Why is this useful?" Structure: bolded/short question headers followed by 2-4 sentence answers; figures/tables referenced inline ("Version information can be found in figure 1.1.").

## Cross-cutting observations

- Constant across every context: plain vocabulary over Latinate where possible, dashes/colons to introduce lists rather than "such as," and a habit of using questions as structural headers (assignments, notes, research docs) rather than declarative headings.
- Register shifts sharply by audience: contractions are absent in formal papers and IR responses, present in user-facing tutorials and personal notes/comments. Sentence length compresses from long multi-clause (formal essays) to fragment/noun-phrase (personal notes) to short imperative (tutorials).
- A vowel-drop/transposition typo pattern recurs across unrelated documents and years: "Whis" for "Why," "descrpt" for "decrypt," "dictionry" for "dictionary," "ecrypted" for "encrypted," "invintory" for "inventory," "Tranfered" for "Transferred" (Work/notes.txt, not quoted per hard rules). Stronger authorship marker than vocabulary — repeats identically across courses, years, and languages (Python vs. Perl).
- What marks a piece as generically "student writing" vs. distinctly hers: the presence of a first-person aside, a joke, or an off-topic personal remark ("Basically me just fucking around," "Get you Windows a la mode," "Questions (The Rabbit Hole)") is the clearest tell. Pure bullet-outline notes or filled-in forms (chain of custody form, firewall ACL tables) read as nearly voiceless — mechanically transcribed data with little discretionary phrasing, and were excluded or given low weight for that reason.
- ASCII section banners (`>>>> <<<<`) appear independently in both academic code (IASAC-240) and personal/work code (file_age_checker.py, several years apart) — a stable structural tic, not course-specific.
