# Sources: what each kind hides, and how to rate one

Steps 4 and 5. Load when the first candidate is open.

## Vary the kind, not the count

Every class of document has one weakness built into what it is for. Another
class covers it. So ask of a candidate not "is this true" but "what would this
never tell me".

Seven classes, each with what it is good for and what it structurally
withholds.

- **The subject's own site or brochure.** Good for current positioning and what
  it wants known. Withholds anything it would rather you not know. An
  advertisement, so never rest a claim on it alone.
- **A self-supplied profile or directory entry.** Good for names, dates,
  claimed scope. Withholds anything unflattering. Selective by construction,
  and not verification.
- **A mandatory filing or disclosure.** Good for audited figures, pending
  litigation, obligations: material the subject would not have volunteered.
  Withholds motive and anything outside its schedule.
- **A first-party operational record.** Good for what actually happened inside
  that system. Withholds whatever the system does not instrument; its silence
  reads as absence.
- **An independent investigation or trade write-up.** Good for judgment on
  quality, culture, how an organization treats people. Withholds internals it
  could not reach, and carries the writer's angle.
- **A regulator's or auditor's report.** Good for a standard applied by
  somebody with no stake in the answer. Withholds speed: old by the time you
  read it.
- **A person who was there.** Good for motive, sequence, what the record leaves
  out. Withholds accuracy on numbers, dates, anything they inferred rather than
  saw.

Routing that follows. Take subjective matters, meaning quality of management,
culture, and how an organization treats people, from writing the subject did
not control. Take what the subject would rather hide from what it is compelled
to publish. Take sequence and motive from people who were there.
GAO-20-195G, the *GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide*, chapter 9, gives
the general form: prefer the body that created or holds the data over anyone
reporting on it.

## Rate the document on two axes, never one

Two different questions. Collapsing them loses information.

**Originator reliability.** How far this body's output has held up in the past,
independent of what this document says. Originator with a record of accuracy
stays reliable on the day it publishes something wrong.

**Document credibility.** How far this particular document is to be trusted, on
its own. Reliable originator publishes an early estimate; poor originator
publishes a verbatim court transcript.

Copy the matrix form of FM 2-22.3, *Human Intelligence Collector Operations*,
appendix B: a letter for originator reliability, a separate numeral for the
individual document's credibility, a value at each end meaning "cannot be
judged" rather than "poor". Document you cannot rate is not a document you rate
badly.

Rate them separately, on whatever scale you state at the top of the table, and
say which is which. Do not average them into one number: "solid body, weak
document" and "weak body, solid document" average to the same number and are
not the same situation.

Originator's reliability genuinely unknown -> say unknown. Do not fully accept
or fully reject a document of uncertain reliability; carry the uncertainty
forward in its row, the only place it can still change anything.

## Corroboration, and the thing that impersonates it

Two documents agreeing is worth something only if they could have disagreed.

Fill the `Derives` column on every row. Name the document this one took its
facts from, or `none` when it reports its own observation. Do it while the
document is open: visible in the text, invisible an hour later.

Circular reporting is the failure this column exists for. Three trade articles,
one press release, one fact. Signs: identical figures to identical precision,
the same unusual phrasing, the same error, a citation chain that converges.

Two more discounts from Heuer, *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, chapter
10, both worth applying at collection time rather than hoping someone catches
them later.

- Vivid single case outweighs aggregate data in the reader's head. Record it,
  record the aggregate next to it.
- One anecdote worth little unless known to be typical. Say whether you know
  that or not.

Claim critical to the eventual answer -> say in its row whether a document
deriving from something else corroborates it, and say plainly when none does.
Critical claim resting on one uncorroborated document is not a reason to stop.
It is a reason to write it down.

## What to do with a document you reject

Give it a consulted-log line saying why. "Only current state, no history" and
"paywalled, title and abstract only" are both useful to the next run.
