# style.md: sentences, numbers, and uncertainty

Sentence layer, numbers rules, table typography, likelihood ladder, weasel
list. Load when drafting or revising sentences, placing numbers, or stating
how sure you are.

first, then the fix. Uncertainty rules pair themselves: weasel phrase is the
bad half, ladder term is the good half.

## The sentence layer

Carter A. Daniel, Part II. Each rule is a bad half then a good half. Good half
is the one to write.

- Active verbs over be-verbs. "I am in favor of" becomes "I favor".
- Active voice over passive. "The stock was bought by us" becomes "We bought
  the stock".
- Kill "-tion" nominalizations; the active verb hides inside them. "reach a
  decision" becomes "decide"; "conduct an organization of" becomes "organize".
- Kill "there is" and "there are". "There are three risks that block this"
  becomes "Three risks block this".
- Specific words over general. "The change affected several areas" becomes
  "The merger cut two departments and closed one plant".
- Simple tough words over Latinate ones; common German-rooted words over
  dandified Latin-based ones. "utilize" becomes "use"; "commence" becomes
  "begin".
- Parallel form in every series. "The plan is cheap, fast, and it will work"
  becomes "The plan is cheap, fast, and reliable".
- Put "only" and "not" beside the word they modify. "He only lost two
  accounts" becomes "He lost only two accounts".
- "this", "that", and "which" must point at something specific. "This shows
  the risk" becomes "This drop in margin shows the risk".
- Do not overqualify. "I would agree that the site is unsuitable" becomes "I
  agree the site is unsuitable".
- No cliches; they substitute for thought. "at the end of the day" becomes the
  actual point.
- No slashes for alternatives. "the cost/benefit" becomes "the cost and the
  benefit"; write "or" or "and".
- Vary sentence length. A run of same-length sentences reads as a drone. Open
  some with a verb, an adverb, or an introductory phrase.
- One pronoun per company, held throughout. Call a company "it" or call it
  "they", pick one, and never switch inside the report.
- Data plural is a usage war with no winner. Route around it: write
  "statistics are" and "information is", never "data is" or "data are".

### The passive-voice detector

From Tongue and Quill, drafting chapter [TQ-7s / `cfd79ca2ffd5`], quoted
verbatim:

> How can you diagnose passive voice? ... find the actor by asking, "Who's
> doing it?" If the actor comes after the verb, it's passive voice. Also,
> watch for these forms of the verb to be (am, is, are, was, were, be, being,
> been) and a main verb usually ending in -ed or -en.

Rule in two moves: find the verb, then find the actor. Actor after the verb,
or a form of "to be" sitting with a main verb ending in -ed or -en -> passive.
Three cures, each a verbatim pair:

- Put the actor before the verb. "The part must have been broken by the
  handlers" becomes "The handlers must have broken the part".
- Drop part of the verb. "The results are listed in the attachment" becomes
  "The results are in the attachment".
- Change the verb. "The replacement has not been received yet" becomes "The
  replacement has not arrived yet".

Passive is allowed only where the actor is unknown or genuinely does not
matter.

### Sentence length target

Same source [TQ-7s / `cfd79ca2ffd5`], verbatim: "You should avoid long,
complicated sentences over 20 words (average is 17 words)." Aim for an average
near 17 words. Treat anything over 20 as a sentence to split.

## The three Strunk supplements

Supplements to the Daniel layer, which has no equivalent for these three.
Strunk, *The Elements of Style*, 1918; every pair below copied verbatim from
the source files. Three, no more.

### Supplement 1: put statements in positive form

Strunk, Chapter III, rule 11 [Strunk hash `3baba3e781e7`, lines 283 to 331].
Verbatim precept: "Make definite assertions. ... Use the word _not_ as a means
of denial or in antithesis, never as a means of evasion." Verbatim pairs:

- "He was not very often on time." becomes "He usually came late."
- "He did not think that studying Latin was much use." becomes "He thought the
  study of Latin useless."
- "not honest" becomes "dishonest"; "did not remember" becomes "forgot".

### Supplement 2: place the emphatic words at the end

Strunk, Chapter III, rule 18 [Strunk hash `3baba3e781e7`, lines 779 to 794].
Verbatim precept: "The proper place in the sentence for the word, or group of
words, which the writer desires to make most prominent is usually the end."
Verbatim pair:

- "This steel is principally used for making razors, because of its hardness."
  becomes "Because of its hardness, this steel is principally used in making
  razors."

### Supplement 3: use "very" sparingly

Strunk, Chapter V, entry "Very" [source hash
`38a1e40c2e7e`, lines 512 to 513]. Verbatim, in full: "Use this word
sparingly. Where emphasis is necessary, use words strong in themselves."

Source gives this entry no example pair, so the illustration below is
skill-authored, not Strunk's, and marked so: "The results were very good"
becomes "The results were excellent".

Two further public-domain Strunk pairs, verified against the source files, for
use anywhere in the report:

- "owing to the fact that" becomes "since (because)" [Strunk hash
  `3baba3e781e7`, lines 469 to 470; the good half reads "since (because)" in
  the source].
- "There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground." becomes
  "Dead leaves covered the ground." [Strunk hash `3baba3e781e7`, lines 267 to
  269].

## Numbers rules

Carter A. Daniel, Part II. Pairs.

- Words for one through nine, figures for 10 and up. "3 cats and thirty-five
  shares" becomes "three cats and 35 shares".
- Never open a sentence with a figure. "35 shares changed hands" becomes
  "Thirty-five shares changed hands".
- Write "0.2", never ".2". ".2 percent" becomes "0.2 percent".
- If you write "from", you must write "to". "from 2002-2008" becomes "from
  2002 to 2008".

## Table typography

DESIGN DEFAULT, skill-authored. No source in the corpus teaches table
typography, so this is house convention, not an attributed rule. Keep it
minimal.

- Caption states the table's point, not its topic, and sits above the table.
  Four-level container law applies: a table is a container and announces its
  conclusion. "Table 1: Costs" becomes "Table 1: Option B costs 40 percent
  less than Option A".
- Left-align text columns. Right-align numeric columns so digits line up;
  align on the decimal point where the column has one.
- One header row. No vertical rules. Whitespace and at most a single
  horizontal rule under the header, not a grid.
- Table resting on assumptions carries the caveat in its own caption. Sourced,
  not design; it lives in the uncertainty layer below.

## The likelihood ladder

Owned by this file, published by every report. Report prints the ladder it
uses, because estimative words drift badly between readers: one reader's
"probable" sits where another hears "almost certain". Reader who distrusts the
words has the numbers, and the reverse.

Published ladder is ICD 203's word row A paired with its numeric bands, shown
together [ICD-D / `4b0f9a500133`, lines 88 to 93]. Reproduce this table in the
report:

| Likelihood         | Probability |
|--------------------|-------------|
| Almost no chance   | 01-05%      |
| Very unlikely      | 05-20%      |
| Unlikely           | 20-45%      |
| Roughly even chance| 45-55%      |
| Likely             | 55-80%      |
| Very likely        | 80-95%      |
| Almost certain(ly) | 95-99%      |

ICD 203 gives a second word row, verbatim [ICD-D / `4b0f9a500133`, line 92]:
"Remote | Highly improbable | Improbable (improbably) | Roughly even odds |
Probable (probably) | Highly probable | Nearly certain". Never mix rows within
one judgment. Report mixes them anyway -> ICD 203 requires a disclaimer noting
the terms mark the same probability.

Corroboration only, never published as the ladder: Kent's odds table [WEP-odds
/ `fd7e456fe8be`, lines 9 to 21] gives certainty 100%, almost certain 93 give
or take 6, probable 75 give or take 12, chances about even 50 give or take 10,
probably not 30 give or take 10, almost certainly not 7 give or take 5,
impossibility 0. Kent's own note on the residual, verbatim [WEP-odds, line
23]: when we say "unlikely" the "chances of its HAPPENING are about one in
four"; "almost certain ... not" still leaves "an appreciable chance, say five
percent or less". State the residual where it would change a decision.

Likelihood and confidence never share a sentence [ICD-D / `4b0f9a500133`,
lines 99 to 102]. "High confidence that it is very likely" reads as one claim
and is two.

## The weasel list

Kent, *Words of Estimative Probability*. Word list, not a category. Each entry
carries a flavor of odds while committing to none, or blurs who observed a
thing. Scan the draft for these, replace each with a ladder term or a plain
source attribution.

Odds-flavored, committing to nothing [WEP-weasel / `aeb42b8d8272`, line 7;
WEP-aes / `1b1b76ab767d`, lines 7 and 9]:

- "serious possibility"
- "distinct possibility"
- "just possible"
- "may well"
- "doubtless"

Kent, verbatim on why they exist [WEP-weasel / `aeb42b8d8272`, line 7]: an
"expression which conveys a definite meaning but at the same time either
absolves us completely of the responsibility or makes the estimate at enough
removes from ourselves as not to implicate us".

Never modify "possible" [WEP-poss / `cd13aa254952`, line 13], verbatim: "The
word 'possible' (and its cognates) must not be modified. The urge to drop into
ordinary usage and write 'just possible,' 'barely possible,' 'a distinct [or
good] possibility,' and so on must be suppressed." Bare "possible" is reserved
for a thing that is neither certain nor impossible and cannot be pinned
tighter; it signals to the reader that contingency planning may be needed.

Source-blurring words [WEP-weasel / `aeb42b8d8272`, line 9], verbatim run:
"apparently", "seemingly", "appears", "seems", "suggests", "indicates",
"presumably", "ostensibly", and "most serious of all, 'reportedly' otherwise
unmodified". Bare "reportedly" "carries no evaluative weight whatsoever". Each
generalizes away who observed the thing and how much the observation weighs.
Use sparingly, never near a key estimate.

Do not double up probability markers [WEP-notes / `9c7195c6fd33`; inventory
rule 200]. "We believe it is likely" stacks two. Adverb carries the odds; "we
believe" and "we estimate" only soften tone. One marker per judgment.

### The uncertainty pair

Rules pair themselves. Weasel phrase is the bad half, ladder term is the good
half:

- "it would appear costs may possibly rise" becomes "costs are likely (55 to
  80 percent) to rise".
- "there is a serious possibility of default" becomes "default is roughly an
  even chance (45 to 55 percent)".

### When a thing genuinely cannot be estimated

Do not refuse and do not weasel. Structure your way through it [WEP-noest /
`5577ed051889`; inventory rule 203]:

- State the contingencies the answer hangs on.
- Give ranges instead of point figures.
- Relabel the output as assumptions for planning, marked as assumptions.
- Put the caveat in the table's own caption, so it travels with the numbers.

## Connectives

Daniel's sentence-connective chapter is the mechanism behind "every sentence
relates to the ones before it". Tagwords tell the reader in advance what is
coming: "but" warns of a turn, "for example" warns of an instance, "also"
warns of one more item in a list.

Revision sweep looks for the three places a connective is missing: where a
paragraph turns, where it gives an example, where it adds to a list. Missing
tagword -> reader reads the sentence twice to learn its job. Three-pass edit
that runs this sweep: references/checklist.md.
